## What ticket proxies actually do—and what they do not do

A ticket proxy routes your browser connection through another IP address. In the ticketing context, the most useful version is generally a **static ISP proxy**: an address assigned to a real internet-service-provider network that remains assigned to you for the session.

That stability matters because buying a ticket is a sequence, not a single page load:

1. You join a waiting room or queue.
2. You reach the event page.
3. You select available inventory.
4. You enter payment and delivery details.
5. You receive a confirmation.

If the connection switches IP addresses halfway through, the ticketing site may treat that as a changed session and ask you to re-authenticate, rejoin, or verify the activity. A rotating proxy is built for a different job—large-scale requests where changing addresses is intentional. It is generally a poor fit for a single, continuous checkout session.

### A proxy is not a ticket-buying shortcut

The important distinction is worth making before comparing providers. A clean, stable proxy may reduce problems caused by an unsuitable or previously flagged connection. It does **not** make a buyer more entitled to inventory than everyone else in the queue.

In the United States, the BOTS Act prohibits circumventing ticket issuer security measures or purchasing rules, including bypassing maximum purchase limits through technology or fake identities. Ticket platforms also have their own terms, account policies, and anti-fraud systems.

> Use a proxy only for lawful, manual access that follows the ticket seller’s rules, stated purchase limits, and account requirements. Do not use it to automate purchases, create fake accounts, evade security controls, or exceed ticket limits.

That is not just legal housekeeping. It also sets realistic expectations. A stable IP solves one narrow connection problem; it does not override a ticketing platform’s rules or risk checks.
